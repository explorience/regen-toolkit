# Funding Your Gatherings

You want to host your node's first real gathering, and the first question in your head is "how do I pay for it?" That question is a trap. It stalls more local nodes than any other, and it's almost always the wrong place to start.

## Start with the cheapest thing that works

Most community gatherings don't need money. They need people, a space, and a purpose. If you wait until you have funding to start, you'll never start — and the waiting itself drains the energy you'll need later.

The best community events in the world are shared meals, park meetups, and living-room conversations that cost nothing. Money helps with scale and comfort, but it's never the prerequisite. Your most valuable resources are time, skills, and goodwill, none of which need a budget line.

As your node grows, you will want events that cost something: workshops with materials, larger gatherings with food, proper venues, multi-day events with outside speakers. Here's how to fund each level without getting ahead of yourself.

## Tier 1: zero budget

These formats cost nothing and still work.

**Shared meals.** Everyone brings a dish. This spreads both cost and effort, and the act of contributing food creates ownership. People who bring something feel like co-hosts; people who show up to a catered spread feel like guests. Use a shared sign-up (a group chat or a simple list) so you don't end up with six bowls of the same thing, keep one person on the hook for a backup main dish, and ask people to label allergens.

**Free venues.** Public libraries often have free meeting rooms, sometimes with a projector. Parks are free and naturally casual. Member homes create intimacy — rotate hosts so the load is shared. Community centres, mosques, churches, and aligned nonprofits will often lend space if your mission fits theirs. Cafés and pubs frequently let groups use a back room or off-peak hours if everyone buys a drink: a few dollars per person, nothing for the organiser.

**Skill contributions.** Instead of paying for facilitation, teaching, or AV, find members who can give those skills. The professional facilitator runs the governance workshop. The baker brings bread. The person with a speaker brings sound. This costs nothing and deepens investment: contributors become co-creators, not consumers.

## Tier 2: low budget ($10–100)

When you need a little money but not much:

**Member contributions.** Suggest a small amount on a "pay what you can" basis: "Suggested contribution: $10 — all welcome regardless of ability to pay." Never turn anyone away. In communities where even small fees create real barriers, drop the suggestion entirely rather than make it awkward.

**Pass the hat.** At the event, pass a jar or post a QR code for mobile payments. Low pressure, people give what feels right, and a 15–20 person gathering usually covers its own basic costs.

**Monthly dues.** If you gather on a regular rhythm, small monthly dues create a predictable fund. Twenty members at a modest amount each month covers most ongoing gatherings without you ever passing a hat.

A small budget buys food and drinks for 15–20 people, printed workshop materials, a 2–3 hour venue rental, supplies for a hands-on activity, or a small honorarium for a local facilitator.

> 🔧 **For practitioners:** Collecting contributions on-chain is the easiest real introduction to wallets your members will ever get. A shared **multisig** wallet — one that needs several people's approval to move funds — or a fundraising tool like [Juicebox](https://juicebox.money/) turns "how do we manage our money?" from abstract to concrete. Even gathering $5 in USDC per person is a working lesson in stablecoins and signatures.

## Tier 3: medium budget ($100–500)

For larger events, workshops with materials, or proper venues:

**Local business sponsorship.** Businesses sponsor community events for visibility, and it works best with genuine alignment — a café backing a community dinner, a co-working space hosting a workshop. Start with places you already use; warm relationships convert far better than cold asks. Be specific ("$200 for food for 40 people" beats "sponsorship"), offer something concrete rather than vague "exposure" — a logo on materials, a thank-you on the night, a table, an introduction to your members — and then actually follow through with the credit and photos. Don't pitch businesses you've never visited, and don't promise attendance numbers you're guessing at.

**Small grants.** Most cities have a community foundation that funds local activities, usually with a short application. Municipal arts and culture grants fund gatherings with an educational or cultural angle. If your node has a cooperative structure, cooperative development funds often back member education and outreach.

**Web3-native funding.** A few platforms are built exactly for this:

- **[Gitcoin Grants](https://grants.gitcoin.co/)** runs funding rounds using **quadratic funding** — a matching formula that rewards the *number* of contributors, not just the dollars, so 100 people giving $1 can out-earn one person giving $100. Gitcoin's current rounds (the GG24 cycle) mix quadratic funding with other allocation mechanisms across specific domains, so check which round fits before applying.
- **[Giveth](https://giveth.io/)** is a zero-fee crypto donation platform. List your project, and supporters from the wider regen community can donate; Giveth also supports recurring donations streamed through Superfluid on Optimism and Base.
- **[Endaoment](https://endaoment.org/)** is an on-chain 501(c)(3) and donor-advised-fund platform. If you have charitable status or a fiscal sponsor, it lets crypto donors give tax-deductibly. Creating a fund is free; donors pay a one-time 1.5% admin fee.

If your node already has a treasury, fund gatherings from it. Events build the social infrastructure everything else depends on — a treasury that never spends on people is a balance sheet with no community attached.

## Tier 4: large budget ($500+)

For multi-day events or gatherings of 50+, you'll likely need $1,000–5,000 or more, which means structured fundraising:

- **Ticket sales.** Even small fees add up; always offer free or reduced tickets, and consider a "pay it forward" option where those who can afford more cover others.
- **Tiered sponsors.** Organise sponsorship into levels (Gold/Silver/Bronze) with escalating visibility.
- **Speaker budgets.** Budget travel and honoraria for outside speakers — $200–500 each is normal for community events. For local speakers, even a token honorarium signals respect.
- **Ecosystem grants.** Apply 3–6 months ahead. The [Ethereum Foundation Ecosystem Support Program](https://esp.ethereum.foundation/) explicitly supports community events and has run requests for proposals to establish permanent community hubs. Other ecosystem and protocol grant programs fund education events too.

A rough 50-person event budget at three levels:

| Item | Low | Medium | High |
|------|-----|--------|------|
| Venue (4 hrs) | $0 (donated) | $200 | $500 |
| Food & drinks | $200 | $400 | $800 |
| Materials/printing | $50 | $100 | $200 |
| Speaker honoraria | $0 (community) | $200 | $500 |
| AV/tech | $0 (borrowed) | $100 | $300 |
| Contingency | $50 | $100 | $200 |
| **Total** | **$300** | **$1,100** | **$2,500** |

## Be transparent about money

Whatever the budget, show the money. Community trust erodes fast when finances are opaque. Share the planned budget before the event and the actual spend after. Decide together what to do with any surplus. Never let one person handle funds without accountability to the group.

This is where Web3 tools genuinely help. An on-chain treasury with multisig approval means no single person can move money without others signing off — that's not just good technology, it's good governance. The habit of transparency on a $200 event is the same habit that lets you manage a real treasury later.

## Don't outspend your community

The hard part isn't funding one event. It's a model that survives. Communities that lead with an expensive, polished launch struggle when the budget runs out. Communities that start with free shared meals and add funded events as they grow build a rhythm that lasts.

This mirrors how any healthy organisation grows: start lean, prove the model, then invest. A node that's gathered for free for six months has the credibility to ask for $500 for a workshop. A node that opens with a $5,000 event and then can't sustain a monthly meetup has a problem. The point was never the catering or the venue — it's whether people leave more connected than they arrived. That part is free.

## Try This

> **Start here:** Before your next gathering, answer three questions in writing — What's the minimum budget that achieves our purpose? Where can a skill contribution replace cash? How will we report what we spent? Most events shrink their own budget once you do this.
>
> **Go deeper:** Set up a shared multisig wallet for your node and collect a small contribution in USDC from each member for one event. Treat it as a teaching exercise as much as a fundraiser, and write down where everyone got stuck — that's your onboarding guide for next time.
>
> **Stretch:** Build a six-month funding ladder. Two zero-budget gatherings to build the habit, one low-budget event funded by member contributions, one medium event with a single local sponsor, and one larger event backed by a small grant or treasury allocation. Track what actually happened against the plan, so you end with real data on your community's economics instead of guesses.
