# Legal Structures for Your Local Node

You've got fifteen people, a shared wallet with a few thousand dollars in it, and a public event next month. Then someone asks the question that quietly stalls a lot of local nodes: "Wait — if something goes wrong, who's actually on the hook?"

The honest answer, if you've done nothing, is: you. And everyone else whose name is on the group chat. This article walks you through the legal structures that fix that, what each one costs in 2026, and how to pick one without losing a season to lawyers.

A quick definition first. A **legal wrapper** is a registered entity — a co-op, a nonprofit, an LLC, or a DAO-specific structure — that sits "around" your group so the entity, not you personally, holds the money, signs the contracts, and absorbs the liability. **Liability** just means legal responsibility for harm or debt. Without a wrapper, your community *is* you, legally speaking.

## Why this stops people (and why it shouldn't stop you)

The fear is real. Without a wrapper, an informal group is usually treated as a **general partnership** — a default legal status where every member can be held personally responsible for the whole group's debts and actions. As Legal Nodes puts it, in an unregistered DAO "each member (each general partner) has unlimited legal liability for all activities conducted by the organization" [Legal Nodes].

That sounds terrifying. But notice the trigger: *activities*. If you're only running discussions and nobody's money or safety is at stake, your exposure is near zero. The paperwork matters exactly when the stakes rise — and at that point, a few hundred dollars of filing beats a lawsuit you pay out of your own savings.

So don't treat this as a wall to clear before you start. Treat it like a fence you put up once your garden has something worth protecting.

## First, size up your actual risk

Match the structure to the risk, not to how serious you want to look. Most nodes overspend here out of nervousness.

**Low risk — you can stay informal.** You facilitate conversations, no money changes hands, you sign nothing, you manage no space. A reading group on regenerative finance lives here. So does a Discord that only coordinates.

**Medium risk — get a simple structure.** You hold small pooled funds, collect membership dues, make micro-grants to local projects, or run events in public spaces. This is where most active **ReFi** (regenerative finance — using crypto and Web3 tools to fund ecological and social good) nodes land within their first year.

**High risk — you need real protection.** You manage a meaningful treasury, pay contractors, hold or rent property, or make investment decisions on others' behalf. Here, going unwrapped is genuinely reckless.

Be honest about which row you're in *today*, not where you hope to be in three years. You can always upgrade.

## Staying informal (the unincorporated association)

This is where almost every group starts: people agreeing to work together, with no registration and no separate legal entity. Neighbourhood watch groups, book clubs, and many community gardens run this way for years.

It works while you're small, money-free, and low-risk. It stops working the moment you collect funds, make decisions about other people's money, or invite the public to something that could go wrong. The protection it offers is zero — that's the trade for zero paperwork.

If you're staying informal, do one thing anyway: keep written records of who decided what and when. Minutes cost nothing and protect everyone if a question arises later.

## The cooperative

A **cooperative** is a business owned and democratically controlled by its members, usually on a one-member-one-vote basis. It's one of the oldest community-enterprise forms on earth — credit unions, REI, and CSA (community-supported agriculture) farms are all co-ops.

For a regen node, the appeal is structural alignment: member-owned and member-governed is the same value you're probably trying to embody on-chain. Co-ops can hold funds, earn income, and distribute surplus back to members, with an established legal framework behind them.

The costs are modest — roughly **$50 to a few hundred dollars** to register in most US states, varying by jurisdiction. The trade-off is discipline: real meetings, real records, and annual filings. Some countries also lack a clean co-op statute, so check yours.

> 🔧 **For practitioners:** If you want co-op governance *and* outside investment, look at the **Limited Cooperative Association (LCA)** — a hybrid that recognises both "patron members" and "investor members" and blends co-op principles with LLC-style liability protection [Wikipedia: LCA]. a16z's DAO legal framework flags LCAs, alongside UNAs and LLCs, as a strong base for decentralized operations [a16z]. In the UK, the closest cousin is the **Community Interest Company (CIC)** — a limited company with a built-in "asset lock" that legally ring-fences assets for community benefit, with dividends capped at 35% of distributable profits [Wikipedia: CIC].

## The nonprofit

A **nonprofit corporation** exists for a public benefit rather than to make money for owners. In the US, the gold standard is **501(c)(3)** status — the IRS tax-exempt category that makes donations tax-deductible for your givers and unlocks most grant funding.

This is the right call when your node's core purpose is charitable or educational: environmental restoration, public-goods education, community resilience. You get tax exemption, grant eligibility, liability protection, and a clear mission lane. The cost is that you *can't* distribute profits to members, your activities must match your stated mission, and political activity is restricted.

The money and time, current as of 2026: the IRS **Form 1023-EZ** (for groups projecting under $50,000/year) costs **$275** and is usually processed in two to four weeks; the full **Form 1023** costs **$600** and typically takes three to six months [IRS]. You can run activities while you wait.

## The LLC

A **Limited Liability Company (LLC)** is a flexible hybrid: it gives you the liability shield of a corporation without most of the formality. Members aren't personally on the hook for the entity's debts, management can be loose, and profits pass through to members' personal taxes (no corporate double-taxation).

Reach for an LLC when your node earns real income, signs contracts, or makes investments and you want strong protection fast. It's the workhorse for investment clubs and any group that needs to *do business*.

Costs vary by state. Wyoming, a popular choice, charges a minimum **$60 annual report fee**, plus a registered agent (anywhere from $0 if you serve as your own, up to around $200/year for a service) [Wyoming SOS; State Business Compliance]. Other states run higher — California's annual franchise tax is $800, for example.

## DAO-specific wrappers (the part that changed recently)

Here's where a draft from a year ago goes stale. Several jurisdictions now offer structures built specifically for **DAOs** (decentralized autonomous organizations — member-governed groups that run partly through on-chain voting and smart contracts). If your node already votes on-chain or holds a treasury in a multisig, these let you keep that *and* gain legal personality.

**Wyoming DUNA — the big one for regen nodes.** The **Decentralized Unincorporated Nonprofit Association**, effective July 1, 2024, is purpose-built for member-governed, mission-driven on-chain groups [Global Fintech & Digital Assets]. Members aren't personally liable for the association's actions, and a judgment against the DUNA doesn't reach individual members [a16z; Fintech & Digital Assets]. Crucially for a regen node, a DUNA is "nonprofit" but can still pay reasonable compensation and run revenue-generating activity. The catch: it requires **at least 100 members** and meaningful on-chain governance, so it suits a maturing node, not a five-person start. The Uniswap Foundation's **DUNI**, proposed in 2025, is one of the first major adopters — a useful real-world reference [Uniswap Foundation].

**Wyoming DAO LLC.** Wyoming was the first US state to recognise DAO LLCs, back in 2021. It marries on-chain governance with LLC liability protection and carries the same low fees as a standard Wyoming LLC (that **$60 minimum** annual report plus a registered agent). Good for smaller treasuries that aren't yet at 100 members.

**Marshall Islands DAO LLC.** Available in for-profit and non-profit flavours via **MIDAO**, the main provider. It's recognised and battle-tested but not cheap — MIDAO's incorporation starts around **$9,500** [MIDAO]. For-profit RMI DAO LLCs pay a 3% gross-revenue tax; non-profit ones are tax-free [Offshore Companies International].

**Cayman Islands foundation.** Common for international projects wanting a foundation structure with DAO governance. Setup runs around **US$2,250**, with annual government fees that rose at the start of 2025 [Offshore Companies International; Cayman General Registry]. Heavier and pricier to maintain; best for sizeable, cross-border treasuries.

The honest practitioner's take: **don't reach for an offshore foundation for a node holding $3,000.** For most local regen nodes, the real choice is co-op vs. nonprofit vs. (once you're big and on-chain) a Wyoming DUNA.

## A decision path you can actually follow

1. **Holding money or signing contracts?** No → stay informal, keep records. Yes → keep going.
2. **Main goal is charitable or educational, and you want grants?** → Nonprofit (501(c)(3); start with 1023-EZ if you're small).
3. **Member-owned, surplus shared back, business-like?** → Cooperative (or LCA / UK CIC).
4. **Earning income or making investments, want fast protection?** → LLC.
5. **100+ members, governing on-chain, mission-driven?** → Wyoming DUNA.
6. **Large cross-border treasury?** → Then, and only then, talk to a Web3 lawyer about Marshall Islands or Cayman.

## The three mistakes that actually hurt people

**Mixing personal and project funds.** This is the single fastest way to lose your liability shield — courts can "pierce" it if the entity and you are financially indistinguishable. Use the entity's name and its own account for everything.

**Skipping annual filings.** Most entities owe an annual report. Miss it and you can be fined or dissolved, quietly losing the protection you paid for. Put the date in a calendar the day you register.

**Over-wrapping too early.** A DUNA or a Cayman foundation for a tiny node is wasted money and overhead — the exact kind of busywork that burns out organizers. Start at the lightest structure your risk allows and upgrade when reality demands it.

## Where to get help for free

You don't have to figure this out alone, and you mostly shouldn't pay a lawyer at the start:

- **Cooperative development centers** offer free co-op formation help in many regions.
- **Legal aid clinics** and law-school clinics often advise nonprofits at no cost.
- **Small Business Development Centers** (US) help with LLCs for free.
- **Web3 legal specialists** are worth the money — but only once your treasury and member count justify it.

A legal structure is a tool to protect your community, not a hoop to jump through before you're allowed to begin.

## Try This

> **Start here:** Run a 20-minute "risk read" with your group. Write down three things: how much money you hold, what you've signed, and whether the public can get hurt at anything you run. That single conversation tells you which row — low, medium, high — you're actually in.
>
> **Go deeper:** Pick your top two structures from the decision path and price them out for *your* jurisdiction. Get the real filing fee, the annual fee, and the processing time from the official source (your Secretary of State, the IRS, your co-op council). Put the numbers side by side — the right answer usually becomes obvious.
>
> **Stretch:** If you're past 100 members and governing on-chain, draft a one-page memo comparing a Wyoming DUNA to your current setup: what liability gap it closes, what it costs to maintain, and what governance changes you'd need. Read the Uniswap Foundation's DUNI proposal first as a worked example, then take your memo to a Web3 lawyer for a paid hour of review.
